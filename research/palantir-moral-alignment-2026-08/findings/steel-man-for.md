# The Steel Man FOR Palantir

The strongest honest case a defender can make, built only from claims that survived verification. Where an argument depends on something unverifiable, that is stated rather than hidden. This case deliberately disowns the pro-Palantir folklore (bin Laden, prevented-attacks claims)—see `fact-check.md`—because a case carrying debunked claims gets discredited wholesale.

## 1. The diagnosis behind the company is correct, and even hostile reviewers concede it

The core argument of Karp and Zamiska's *The Technological Republic*: Silicon Valley was born from state-directed defense investment (DARPA, radar, GPS), abandoned that inheritance for advertising and engagement optimization, and left the state technologically hollow at exactly the moment software became the decisive military technology. The book's sharpest critics grant the diagnosis. Tanner Greer, reviewing it harshly from the right, concedes "the problem of the unmoored engineer" is real. The academic and libertarian reviews dispute the prescriptions, not the premise. If the premise is right, someone with elite engineering talent has to do this work.

## 2. Someone builds this either way, and the realistic alternative is worse

The demand for state data integration comes from elected governments executing congressionally authorized programs. The honest comparison is not Palantir versus no surveillance; it is Palantir versus the same programs running on unlogged legacy databases, spreadsheet exports, and email chains—environments where analysts have historically snooped on ex-partners and celebrities without a trace. Government-authored Privacy Impact Assessments for Palantir-based systems cite named-user audit logging and granular access control as the mitigations that make Privacy Act compliance verifiable. This is the strongest technical argument in the case: immutable audit logs make abuse discoverable in a way the legacy baseline never did, and it is corroborated by the government's own privacy documentation rather than by Palantir marketing.

## 3. The verified operational record has real moral weight

- **Ukraine:** first-hand Washington Post reporting documents Ukrainian officers running Palantir software in live targeting workflows; named Ukrainian ministers attest to demining work under the national mine-action strategy (156,000 km² potentially contaminated), war-crimes evidence collection, and reconstruction uses across more than half a dozen agencies; the software was reportedly provided free. This is material assistance to a democracy under invasion, attested by people with no incentive to flatter a vendor.
- **Public health:** Tiberius allocated COVID vaccines across US jurisdictions during Operation Warp Speed (mechanism documented by MIT Technology Review); NHS England reports large waiting-list and discharge improvements from the Federated Data Platform—self-reported and causally uncontrolled, with an independent evaluation commissioned, but not nothing.
- **Child protection:** a partnership with the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children since 2010, supporting analysts processing tens of thousands of reports daily.
- **Most of the company is unglamorous:** ~46% of revenue is commercial—hospitals, energy, manufacturing. NATO bought the Maven system in 2025. The company critics describe is half the company.

## 4. Palantir holds lines its critics ignore

A categorical refusal to sell to China or Russia, disclosed in SEC filings where misstatement carries legal exposure, maintained at real commercial cost. A privacy and civil liberties engineering function built around 2010, roughly a decade before the industry norm, staffed by named people with real publication records. Signed public rebuttals to the UN, Amnesty, the New York Times, and Congress—engagement with critics that most defense contractors never attempt. And when the Team Themis scandal broke in 2011 (a proposal to attack WikiLeaks and pressure a journalist), Palantir severed the relationship and Karp apologized personally and by name—arguably the most accountable behavior in the company's history.

## 5. Democratic legitimacy

Enforcement priorities are set by elected administrations and authorized by Congress. The vendor executing lawful policy is not the moral author of the policy; the electorate is. And the system's correction mechanisms demonstrably operate: a German constitutional court struck down the statutes behind a Palantir deployment, New Orleans ended its program under press scrutiny, the LAPD terminated LASER after its Inspector General audited it. (The defender should concede these corrections were triggered by journalists, courts, and auditors rather than by routine oversight—but they happened, which is what functioning accountability looks like from outside.)

## 6. Deterrence, and the humanitarian version of precision

If great-power war with China is the century's central risk, a decisive Western military-software advantage is among the few things that plausibly prevents it. The morally serious version of the lethality argument is about discrimination: better targeting intelligence should mean fewer civilians harmed per unit of military effect, making precision software a humanitarian technology. Honesty requires the flag: no independent study has measured a civilian-casualty reduction attributable to Palantir; this is the highest-value unverified claim in the defender's case. But the direction of the argument is sound, and its unverifiability is a property of classified warfare, not evidence of falsity.

## What the defender must concede

To remain a steel man rather than marketing, this case concedes: the counterterrorism outcome claims are unfalsifiable; "we only serve democracies" is false (the Gulf business exists and Karp acknowledges the ethical difference); the 2020-to-2025 reversal on ICE deportation-operations work happened and the company has never explained what changed; the accountability arguments in the against-case attack real facts, not caricatures.
